What kind of company this is.

Before logo, color, or type — one question. What do an airport and its concessionaires actually need to run smoothly behind the scenes? ALM is the answer to that question. Everything else in this book exists to say it plainly, on every surface, without ever overpromising.

01 / The Definition

One word. Taken literally.

ALM's brand begins with a dictionary entry. Not a slogan, not a mission statement — a definition. It has opened the homepage since 2017 and it is the single most ALM thing ever written: the company promises exactly what the word means, then does it every morning before the terminal opens.
lo·gis·tics
ləˈjistiks/
noun
The detailed coordination of a complex operation involving many people, facilities, or supplies.
— Why this device works

Every competitor claims to be a partner, a solution, an innovator. ALM claims to be the definition of logistics — nothing more, nothing less. The dictionary entry is the brand's founding move: plain words, precisely used, with the proof rolling across the ramp at Dulles since 2005. Keep it. It opens the website, it can open a proposal, and it sets the bar for every sentence that follows.

02 / The Answer

What ALM actually does.

Airports are small cities with tight security. Hundreds of trucks delivering to individual restaurants and shops would clog the behind-the-scenes traffic of any terminal. ALM replaces that chaos with one secure, central facility — and runs it, every day, for the airport and every concessionaire in it.
— The ALM Why · Brand Foundation
One secure dock. Every delivery received, tracked, and rolled to every concessionaire — before the airport wakes up.
The line underneath every word in this book: we coordinate the complex operation so the airport doesn't have to.
Pillar · 01
Comfortable.

A concessionaire should relax the moment ALM takes the handoff. The brand feels like that moment: calm surfaces, soft edges, no alarm colors, nothing shouting.

Pillar · 02
Safe & secure.

Badged crew, vetted escorts, TSA-governed space. The brand signals security the way the operation does — quietly, as a standard, never as a scare tactic.

Pillar · 03
Fairly priced.

Logistics should never eat a tenant's margin. The brand talks about money plainly — a 60% cost reduction, stated in numbers — and never dresses itself up to look expensive.

Pillar · 04
Efficient.

2,000 pieces a day through one building, scanned in and scanned out. The brand moves the same way: short sentences, clear pages, no wasted motion.

05 / Brand Principles

Five principles. One mark.

Each principle ties back to a feature of the ALM wordmark — the extended crossbar, the forward lean, the single line weight, the workwear navy, and the three-letter economy. The mark is the operation compressed into a shape.
Principle 01The Runway

Service that extends.

The A's crossbar runs out past the letter like a runway threshold — the mark starts before the name does. So does the company. Deliveries are received, processed, and rolling toward the concourse before the airport wakes up. ALM begins where the concessionaire's day hasn't started yet, and that head start is the entire value of the service.

Principle 02The Lean

Always in motion.

Every stroke in the wordmark drives forward at the same angle. Nothing in the mark stands still, because nothing in the operation does — 2,000 pieces a day move through one building and out to 50 doors. The brand never sounds parked: prefer verbs of movement (receive, scan, deliver, track) over verbs of posture (position, leverage, enable).

Principle 03The Monoweight

The same weight, every day.

One stroke width across all three letters. No flourishes, no thick-and-thin theatrics. That is the promise of the operation: Tuesday gets the same delivery as opening day. The voice matches — it does not spike with excitement or sag into apology. Construction season, TSA rule changes, snowstorms: the tone stays level because the trucks still run.

Principle 04The Navy

Calm is the color.

The crew wears navy because navy reads as steady, trustworthy, and on-duty — it never asks for attention and never causes alarm. The brand wears it the same way. The palette comes off the crew's backs and the side of the trucks, and every other color in the system exists only to make the navy feel calmer, clearer, and easier to read.

Principle 05The Three Letters

Short, because time matters.

Airport Logistics Management Services compresses to three letters on the side of a truck, because a dock manager radioing a gate doesn't have time for nine syllables. The writing follows the same rule. Answer first, detail after. If a sentence can lose a word, it loses the word.

07 / Voice in Practice

How ALM writes.

Short sentences that respect a busy reader. Concrete images — small cities, tight security, trucks at the dock. Below, before-and-after pairs for the situations ALM writes into most often. The "do" column is built from language the company already uses.
✕  Don't — Homepage hero
"ALM is a leading provider of innovative, end-to-end supply chain solutions, leveraging best-in-class processes to deliver world-class value for our aviation partners."
Five buzzwords, zero facts. It could be any vendor in any industry. Nothing tells an airport director or a concessionaire what ALM actually operates, or where, or since when.
✓  Do — Homepage hero
"lo·gis·tics — noun. The detailed coordination of a complex operation involving many people, facilities, or supplies."
The signature move: claim the definition, then prove it. Confident enough to need no adjectives, and it sets up everything that follows on the page.
✕  Don't — Service description
"Our centralized receiving solution optimizes multi-tenant inbound logistics flows through a synergistic cross-docking paradigm."
The reader has to translate every clause. Jargon makes a simple, comforting idea sound complicated — the opposite of what ALM sells.
✓  Do — Service description
"Airports are like small cities with tight security. That's why they need one central facility for deliveries — away from the terminal, where supplier trucks deliver their goods, and ALM takes them the rest of the way."
A picture, then a reason, then the service. This is real ALM homepage copy — it explains cross-docking to a stranger in three sentences without one piece of jargon.
✕  Don't — The cost case
"Our value engineering delivers significant TCO improvements and operational efficiencies across the logistics value chain."
"Significant" is doing all the work and it isn't a number. A claim without a figure reads as a claim without proof — and to a small concessionaire, vague usually means expensive.
✓  Do — The cost case
"For airports and tenants, logistics is not a profit center. It's a profit destroyer. With ALM, you can count on at least a 60% reduction in logistics cost — a six-figure saving for the airport."
A blunt diagnosis, then a hard number. The "profit destroyer" line is the sharpest sentence ALM has ever published. Keep writing like that.
✕  Don't — Team bio
"A passionate, visionary logistics leader dedicated to driving transformational change and inspiring operational excellence."
"Passionate" and "visionary" communicate nothing about competence. Nobody at a loading dock has ever been described this way by a colleague.
✓  Do — Team bio
"Mark's work as Operations Manager means he's trained extensively in warehouse systems and delivery & receiving distribution — and supervises loading and unloading at secure airport terminals."
What he does, what he's trained in, where he does it. Facts a client can verify, written the way a reference would say them.
✕  Don't — Call to action
"Unlock your airport's potential today! Schedule a free consultation with one of our logistics experts now!"
Exclamation marks and funnel language. Airport authorities don't "unlock potential" — they award contracts to operators they trust.
✓  Do — Call to action
"Ready to get your airport working more smoothly? Let's get started today. 571‑236‑6294"
One question, one invitation, one phone number a human answers. This is the CTA from the live site — it needs nothing added.

10 / Clear Space & Minimum Size

The mark needs room to move.

Leave a margin equal to the cap-height of the letter A (X) on all four sides. More is always allowed; less never is. Minimum reproduction width: 70px on screen, 25mm in print — below that, the crossbar starts to close up.

Why this matters.

The extended crossbar is what makes the mark feel like motion — a runway running out ahead of the name. Crowd it against copy, another logo, or a page edge, and the runway becomes a fence. The mark earns its meaning from the space it moves through.

On vehicles, the rule doubles: two cap-heights minimum on every side, because livery is read at distance and at speed. When in doubt, double the space.

11 / What Not to Do

Six things never done to the mark.

Each of these breaks the mark in a specific way. If a vendor's proof arrives with one of them, send it back with this page attached. No exceptions for "just this once."
✕
Don't stretch or compress the wordmark. The proportions are fixed.
✕
Don't rotate or angle the lockup. The lean is built into the letters already.
✕
Don't recolor the mark. Black, white, or nothing.
✕
Don't add drop shadows, outlines, glows, or bevels.
✕
Don't place the mark on busy photography without a scrim — and never black-on-photo.
✕
ALM
Don't typeset "ALM" as a substitute for the wordmark artwork.

18 / Imagery Rules

What gets in. What doesn't.

One test decides everything: could a competitor run this image without anyone noticing? If yes, it doesn't belong to ALM. The fleet, the crew, the badges, the unit numbers — that's what makes a photo ours.
✓  Always
  • ALM vehicles with visible unit numbers (ALM 5, ALM 7) and livery.
  • Real crew, mid-task: scanning, loading, escorting, signing.
  • Airside context — tails, towers, jet bridges — shot from authorized positions.
  • Natural daylight or true dusk; the blue-sky-and-steel palette.
  • Security visible and worn correctly: badges, vests, escort positioning.
✕  Never
  • Stock photography of warehouses, handshakes, or "global logistics" globes.
  • Clip-art aircraft or rendered planes. The real ramp is thirty feet away.
  • Filters, heavy HDR, or color grades that fight the navy-and-sky palette.
  • Imagery that reveals security procedures, badge details, or restricted layouts.
  • Passengers, or anything shot in the public terminal without clearance.

19 / Anatomy of a Proof Point

Four parts. Every time.

A testimonial earns its place when it carries all four parts below. A star rating or an unattributed "great service!" carries none of them — and never appears on an ALM surface.
01
A named person

First and last name, real title, real organization. Proof that can't be verified isn't proof.

02
A real operation

The airport, the contract, the timeframe. "Since January 2005" is worth a paragraph of adjectives.

03
A specific act

What ALM actually did — escorted the trucks, lent the equipment, opened four restaurants in three months.

04
A verdict

The sentence that recommends. Quoted exactly as written — never paraphrased, never "improved."

25 / Navigation Language

Plain questions, plain answers.

ALM's navigation answers the three questions every visitor brings, in their own words. This is a brand decision, not a writing accident — defend it against every redesign that proposes "Solutions."
About page
"About Us" / "Our Story" / "Company"
Who We Are — the visitor's question, answered in their words.
Services page
"Solutions" / "Capabilities" / "Offerings"
What We Provide — provision, not "solutioning."
Testimonials
"Clients" / "Success Stories" / "Case Studies"
What People Think — honest enough to imply the answer could have been negative.
Contact
"Get Started" / "Request a Demo"
Contact — one word, one phone number, one form.

26 / Homepage Spec

What the homepage must do.

Eight requirements, in order. Any future rebuild of almservices.com is measured against this list before it is measured against anything else.
— 01 · Hero
Full-bleed ramp photo, white lockup
A real photograph of the operation under the navy scrim, with the full white lockup centered. No carousel, no video background, no headline competing with the mark.
— 02 · The Definition
lo·gis·tics, right after the hero
The dictionary entry on a navy panel — the brand's founding move in its canonical position. It opens the argument; everything below it is evidence.
— 03 · Services
The five services, by their fixed names
Each linking to its full description. Plain icons, no illustration system — the photography carries the visual weight on this site.
— 04 · Numbers
The operation, counted
Since 2005 · 2 airports · 2,000 pieces daily · 41 sites · 60% cost reduction. The six figures from Chapter 01, kept current.
— 05 · Proof
The testimonial wall
Photo tiles with attributed pull-quotes from Chapter 07, each opening the full signed letter. Real names, real titles, real organizations — always.
— 06 · The People
One invitation to meet the team
A single navy band: "Get to know the people behind ALM and how it got started." One button. The operation is human and the site should say so once, plainly.
— 07 · Contact
Phone first
"Ready to get your airport working more smoothly? Let's get started today." Then 571-236-6294, then the form. The phone number is the conversion event — everything else is backup.
— 08 · Weight
Fast on airport Wi-Fi
Compressed photography, system-cached fonts, no tracking bloat. The decision-maker is reading this in a terminal office or an apron vehicle. Performance is brand.
